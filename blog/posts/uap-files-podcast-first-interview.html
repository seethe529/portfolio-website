Someone Finally Listened

July 12, 2026 Reflection 5 min read
Accountability Transparency UAP Interview

On July 8, 2026, I sat down with Jimmy on the UAP Files Podcast and told my story publicly for the first time. After three years of documenting, organizing evidence, filing with inspectors general, writing to congressional offices, and being met largely with silence — someone finally gave me the space to speak.

I'm grateful for that.

⸻

Why Now

I've spent three years building a record. Every email, every filing, every instance of retaliation — documented and archived. I wrote a book about it. I contacted the agencies that were supposed to protect people in my position. Most of that work happened in silence, directed at institutions that acknowledged receipt and then went quiet.

The podcast was different. It was someone saying: tell me what happened. And then actually listening.

Jimmy approached this with genuine curiosity and respect for the details. He let the story breathe. He asked follow-up questions that showed he'd done his homework. That matters when you've spent years wondering if anyone outside the bureaucratic chain would ever hear the full picture.

⸻

What We Covered

The conversation covered the full arc — the tool I built for the FAA that compared pilot-reported UAP sightings against satellite orbital data, the institutional response when that work gained attention, and the retaliation that followed. We talked about what it's like to build something useful and then watch the system turn against you for building it.

We also talked about where I am now. The Orbital Zoning Framework. The book. The decision to keep building rather than disappear. Three years is a long time to carry something like this. Speaking about it publicly doesn't make it lighter, exactly, but it makes the weight feel less solitary.

Why I'm Sharing This Here

This website is my professional portfolio. It's also the most complete record of who I am and what I've built. The podcast interview belongs here because it's part of the story — not separate from the technical work, but connected to it. The same person who built a 3D airspace visualization tool for the FAA is the same person who spent three years trying to get someone to acknowledge what happened afterward.

I'm not angry anymore. I'm reflective. I built the tool. I documented what followed. I wrote the book. And now I've spoken about it publicly. That feels like a complete arc — or at least the end of one chapter of it.

Thank you to Jimmy and the UAP Files Podcast for providing the platform and treating the story with the seriousness it deserves. After years of documentation directed at institutions, being heard by a real person in a real conversation meant more than I expected.
